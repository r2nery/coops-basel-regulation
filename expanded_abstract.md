
# ONE-SIZE-FITS-ALL? COOPERATIVE VS. NON-COOPERATIVE FINANCIAL INSTITUTIONS UNDER FULL BASEL-STYLE REGULATION IN BRAZIL

Arthur Gomes Nery
Sistema OCB

### EXTENDED ABSTRACT

#### Motivation

Basel-style prudential regulation was designed for shareholder-owned banks, institutions that raise equity on capital markets, distribute dividends to attract investors, and adjust their capital structure through market transactions. Credit cooperatives operate under fundamentally different constraints. They are member-owned, democratically governed, and cannot issue shares on public markets. Their primary capital sources are retained earnings and member contributions (quotas-parte). Despite these structural differences, cooperatives in Brazil are increasingly subject to the same prudential framework as banks, following BACEN's progressive regulatory convergence under Resolution 4,553/2017 and subsequent norms. This raises a question with direct policy implications: under identical regulatory rules, do cooperatives maintain a distinct behavioral profile, or does uniform regulation effectively homogenize institutional behavior across organizational forms?

#### Data and Method

We use institution-level quarterly balance sheet, income statement, and asset composition data from BACEN's IF.data system, covering all regulated financial institutions in Brazil from 2014 to 2024 (61,380 institution-quarter observations across 1,959 institutions). We restrict the analysis to institutions operating under full Basel-style prudential methodology, yielding an analysis sample of 15,004 observations from 650 institutions: 168 credit cooperatives and 483 non-cooperative financial institutions.

This restriction is the core of our identification strategy. By comparing cooperatives and non-cooperatives within the same regulatory tier, we hold constant the prudential rules (capital requirements, risk-weighting methodology, reporting standards) and isolate the association between institutional form and financial behavior.

We examine eight balance sheet and income statement outcomes spanning four dimensions of institutional behavior: capital structure (Basel capital adequacy ratio and leverage), profitability (return on assets, net interest margin, and cost-to-income ratio), credit quality (credit portfolio allocation and loan loss provisioning rate), and funding (deposit reliance). To address concerns that size and geography may confound the comparison, we estimate the cooperative differential under four specifications of increasing stringency: (1) unconditional mean differences; (2) OLS controlling for log total assets and calendar-quarter fixed effects; (3) coarsened exact matching (CEM) on asset size quintile and Brazilian macro-region, retaining 599 of 650 institutions, with the same regression controls; and (4) restriction to the common support region where cooperatives and non-cooperatives overlap in the asset distribution (log assets between 11.47 and 15.96), with the same controls. Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are used throughout.

#### Results

Six of eight outcomes produce stable results, maintaining the same sign and statistical significance (p < 0.001) across all four specifications.

On capital structure, cooperatives hold substantially less regulatory capital. The Basel capital adequacy ratio is 7 to 8 percentage points lower for cooperatives (CEM specification: -7.16pp, 95% CI [-7.89, -6.42]). This finding strengthens rather than attenuates under the strictest common-support restriction (-8.07pp). Cooperatives are also 11 to 15 percentage points more leveraged (CEM: +14.89pp, 95% CI [+14.37, +15.42]), reflecting greater reliance on deposit funding relative to equity.

On profitability, cooperatives are slightly more profitable. Return on assets is 0.1 to 0.3 percentage points higher (CEM: +0.28pp, 95% CI [+0.21, +0.35]). However, cooperatives earn a lower net interest margin, ranging from 1.6 to 3.5 percentage points below non-cooperatives (CEM: -3.48pp, 95% CI [-3.67, -3.29]). Cooperatives also operate with lower cost-to-income ratios, with differences ranging from 0.9 to 1.5 points (CEM: -1.51, 95% CI [-1.63, -1.39]). Taken together, these results indicate that cooperatives charge lower intermediation spreads to their members but compensate through greater operational efficiency, resulting in modestly higher overall profitability.

On credit quality, cooperatives exhibit lower provisioning rates. The ratio of loan loss provisions to gross credit is 4 percentage points lower for cooperatives (CEM: -4.08pp, 95% CI [-4.37, -3.79]). This finding is remarkably stable, with the coefficient varying by less than 0.2 percentage points across all four specifications. Lower provisioning rates indicate either better credit quality in the cooperative loan portfolio or more conservative lending practices, and in either interpretation they suggest that the lower capital buffers observed for cooperatives do not translate into higher credit risk.

Two outcomes, credit portfolio allocation and deposit reliance, are not robust. Both show positive cooperative differentials in the full-sample and matched specifications but reverse sign when restricted to the common support region. Diagnostic analysis reveals that within the region of asset overlap, cooperatives and non-cooperatives allocate nearly identical shares of assets to credit (43.7% vs. 43.6%) and non-cooperatives actually rely more on deposits (51.4% vs. 48.4%). The full-sample results on these dimensions reflect composition effects driven by very small non-cooperative institutions with different business models, not a genuine behavioral difference between comparable institutions.

#### Implications

Our findings document that cooperative financial institutions maintain a structurally distinct profile even when subject to identical Basel-style prudential regulation. This distinctiveness spans capital structure, profitability, efficiency, and credit quality, and it persists across multiple comparison strategies including matching on institution size and geographic region.

The pattern of results is internally consistent and points to a specific interpretation. Cooperatives cannot recapitalize through equity markets, which produces lower capital ratios and higher leverage. They compensate through lower intermediation margins (returning value to members), greater operational efficiency (lower cost-to-income), and more conservative or higher-quality lending (lower NPL ratios). The net result is modestly higher profitability despite the capital constraint.

These findings carry implications for the design of prudential regulation. Applying uniform capital benchmarks to institutions with structurally different capital accumulation channels risks penalizing a feature of the cooperative model rather than identifying genuine prudential fragility. The lower capital ratios we document coexist with lower credit risk and higher profitability, a combination that challenges the assumption that capital adequacy ratios have identical informational content across institutional forms. Our evidence supports the case for proportional regulation that accounts for organizational form, consistent with ongoing policy debates at BACEN and in the international cooperative banking literature.
