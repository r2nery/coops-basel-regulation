# One-Size-Fits-All? Credit Cooperatives Under Full Basel Regulation in Brazil

**Keywords:** credit cooperatives, prudential regulation, Basel, cooperative banking, Brazil, proportionality

**Themes:** 12. Cooperative Banks and Mutual Insurers; 8. Measuring Impact and Performance of Cooperatives

**Preferred presentation type:** Oral

---

## Background

Prudential regulation of financial institutions increasingly converges toward a single framework rooted in the Basel Accords. In Brazil, the Central Bank (BACEN) routes institutions into segments and allows smaller, simpler institutions an optional simplified methodology for computing minimum capital, while larger and more complex institutions apply the complete set of Basel-style capital, leverage, and risk requirements regardless of organizational form. Once a credit cooperative crosses the relevant complexity threshold, it computes capital, leverage, and risk exposures under the same full methodology as a commercial bank, an investment bank, or any other comparable non-cooperative institution.

This regulatory convergence raises a question that matters for both policymakers and the cooperative movement: does identical regulation produce identical institutional behavior, or do cooperatives retain a distinct profile? If cooperatives systematically differ from non-cooperatives even under the same rules, then one-size-fits-all benchmarks calibrated to the average regulated institution may misstate the risk and performance characteristics of cooperative banking. Despite growing policy attention to proportionality in financial regulation (Ferri, 2012; Bülbül et al., 2013), empirical evidence on cooperative behavior under full Basel-style regulation remains limited, particularly in emerging markets.

## Aims

This study characterizes behavioral differences between credit cooperatives and non-cooperative financial institutions operating under identical prudential regulation in Brazil. We examine eight outcome dimensions spanning capital adequacy, profitability, efficiency, credit, and funding structure. We assess the robustness of each differential using four specifications of increasing stringency and decompose the sources of attenuation to determine whether observed gaps reflect genuine institutional differences or compositional artifacts driven by size.

## Methods

We construct an institution-level quarterly panel from BACEN's IF.data, covering balance sheets, income statements, asset composition, and prudential segmentation from 2014 to 2024. We classify institutions on the regulator's own consolidation type (*tcb*) rather than on trade names, assigning each its modal type so that cooperative status is time-invariant by construction; singular cooperatives are identified as `b3S` and central cooperatives as `b3C`. The analysis sample restricts to institutions classified under full Basel methodology and, on the cooperative side, to singular retail cooperatives, excluding central cooperatives as wholesale infrastructure entities that are not retail member institutions. This yields 613 institutions (130 singular cooperatives, 483 non-cooperatives) and 13,912 institution-quarter observations spanning 2017Q1 to 2024Q4.

We estimate the cooperative differential using four nested specifications: (1) unconditional comparison; (2) full sample with log assets and calendar-quarter fixed effects; (3) coarsened exact matching (CEM) on size quintile and macro-region with the same controls; and (4) common-support trimming with the same controls. This progressive design reveals how much of each raw gap reflects size composition versus a persistent institutional differential. We compute an attenuation ratio (raw to trimmed) and classify each outcome as robust if sign and significance hold across all four specifications with attenuation below 60 percent, partially robust if they hold but attenuation reaches 60 percent or more, and unstable if sign or significance fails in any specification.

Because cooperative status is time-invariant, institution fixed effects cannot be estimated alongside the cooperative indicator; the design therefore documents conditional correlations rather than causal effects of organizational form. Inference rests on standard errors clustered by institution, with heteroskedasticity-robust (HC3) errors reported alongside; the HC3 intervals are uniformly tighter, so the clustered errors are the conservative basis for inference. Standardized differences confirm that CEM balances the matching covariates (size, geography), while persistent outcome imbalance after matching constitutes the finding itself rather than a design failure.

## Results

Every one of the eight differentials retains its sign and its statistical significance across all four specifications, and none reverses once the comparison is confined to institutions of common size; the unstable category is empty.

Four findings are robust in magnitude as well as sign. Cooperatives hold roughly 8 to 12 percentage points less regulatory capital (Basel ratio), attenuating by only about 21 percent from raw to trimmed. They report lower provisioning ratios, by about 3.3 percentage points, with essentially no attenuation (the trimmed coefficient is marginally larger than the raw one), making this the single most stable result in the study. They operate leaner, with cost-to-income ratios about 3.1 points lower unconditionally and about 1.2 points lower after trimming. And they devote a larger share of assets to credit, higher by about 25 percentage points unconditionally and about 13 points after trimming, an attenuation of roughly half.

Four findings are partially robust: stable in sign and significance but attenuating by more than half once size is held constant. Cooperatives carry more leverage (about 22 percentage points unconditionally, about 9 after trimming); the Basel ratio and leverage are two views of one equity cushion and are strongly negatively correlated among cooperatives (r ≈ −0.84), so they should not be read as independent findings. Return on assets is positive in every specification but modest, falling from about 0.86 percentage points unconditionally to about 0.34 after trimming. The net interest margin is lower for cooperatives throughout, narrowing from about 6.5 to about 1.1 percentage points, the largest attenuation of any outcome. The deposit ratio is higher for cooperatives throughout, from about 28 to about 8 percentage points after trimming.

Splitting cooperatives by integrated system shows that the pooled capital gap is concentrated rather than uniform: it is precisely estimated for Sicredi (about 9.7 percentage points less capital), the same direction but insignificant for Sicoob, and positive but insignificant for the six independent cooperatives, a pattern consistent with, though not establishing, a network-backstop mechanism. Lower provisioning, by contrast, holds and is statistically significant in every system, reinforcing its status as the most robust finding.

## Discussion

The robust results cohere into a recognizable institutional portrait: a focused, deposit-funded, member-lending retail institution that holds lower capital buffers, runs leaner cost structures, devotes a larger share of assets to credit, and reports lower provisioning rates than non-cooperatives of comparable size and location. This is consistent with cooperatives prioritizing member service over capital accumulation, with the operating efficiency a focused retail model can deliver, and with earning a small positive return through volume and cost discipline rather than wide intermediation spreads.

Several interpretive cautions apply. The provisioning measure captures an accounting quantity — the stock of provisions over gross credit — not delinquency; without loan-level default data we cannot determine whether lower provisioning reflects better loan portfolios, more conservative lending, a different portfolio mix, or different provisioning practice, and we deliberately avoid inferring lower credit risk from it. The profitability advantage is real in sign but small and size-sensitive, with about three fifths of the raw gap disappearing once size is held constant. The sample restriction to full-methodology institutions selects the largest and most sophisticated cooperatives — only about 18 percent of active cooperatives qualify in a given quarter, and the 130 singular cooperatives analyzed are roughly a tenth of all cooperatives ever observed — limiting generalizability to the broader cooperative universe. Finally, residual within-group heterogeneity remains, and most systems are too thinly represented under full methodology to support separate inference.

## Contributions and Implications

This study offers three contributions. First, it provides what is, to our knowledge, the most comprehensive characterization of cooperative behavior under full Basel-style regulation in an emerging market, using the full population of full-methodology institutions rather than a convenience sample. Second, the four-specification framework with attenuation decomposition provides a transparent methodology for distinguishing genuine institutional differentials from compositional artifacts. Third, the findings carry direct policy relevance: if cooperatives systematically maintain lower capital buffers while operating with lower cost ratios, lending a larger share of their assets, and provisioning no more than comparable banks, then uniform capital benchmarks calibrated to the average full-methodology institution may not adequately reflect the distinct risk-return profile of cooperative banking. Regulators designing proportional frameworks should account for these structural differences — and, where the evidence supports it, for system membership — rather than treating organizational form as irrelevant once an institution is inside the full-methodology segment.
