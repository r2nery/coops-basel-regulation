\begin{frame}{On the taxation of cooperatives}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.54\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{s_tax_simple}
\vspace{0.05cm}
{\scriptsize\color{muted}Medians with size and quarter controls (2); the tables on earlier slides show averages. After tax: net of tax and profit sharing.\par}
\column{0.44\textwidth}
\vspace{0.3cm}
{\footnotesize For the typical cooperative, most of the after-tax gap is already there before tax. Three things set its surplus apart from a bank's profit:\par}
\vspace{0.15cm}
{\footnotesize\setlength{\leftmargini}{1em}\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{4pt}
\item A credit cooperative lends to and takes deposits from its own owners. The surplus belongs to them, so the law does not treat it as profit earned from others. Operations with non-members are taxed.
\item It is partly what members paid above cost. A larger surplus can mean higher prices to members, returned to them later.
\item What is retained goes largely to mandatory reserves that cannot be distributed. It is the main way a cooperative builds capital.
\end{itemize}\par}
\vspace{0.1cm}
{\tiny\color{muted}Laws 5.764/1971 and 10.865/2004. Median effective tax rate in profitable quarters: banks 35\%, cooperatives~1\%.\par}
\end{columns}
\note{One minute. The prudential rules are the same for both groups; tax is the one rule the framework does not equalise, so we look at profit before and after tax. By law, the results of a cooperative's operations with its own members are outside taxable income. The idea is that the cooperative is its members acting together, so the surplus on those operations is their own money, returned to them or kept in common reserves. What a cooperative does with non-members is taxed as usual. Before tax, the typical cooperative earns 3.2 percent of assets a year and a bank of the same size 1.8. After tax, 2.8 against 1.1. So the cooperative is ahead before any tax is paid; the exemption widens the gap a little. Two things to keep in mind when comparing that with a bank's profit. A cooperative's surplus is partly what members paid above cost, so a larger surplus can mean higher prices to members that come back to them later. And what it keeps goes largely into reserves that cannot be distributed: retained surplus is the main way a cooperative builds capital, since it cannot issue shares to outside investors. The paper estimates that at the banks' tax rate, cooperative capital would accumulate about three tenths more slowly. Whether the exemption is a subsidy or the recognition of mutual self-help is not something these data settle.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Result 2. Capital: the average overstates the typical gap}
\vspace{0.1cm}
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{s_capital_dist}}
\vspace{0.05cm}
{\scriptsize\color{muted}Institution-quarters, 2017 to 2024.\par}
\vspace{0.2cm}
{\centering\small A tail of banks that are small or barely lend, with ratios above 50 percent, pulls the bank mean up. Considering typical institutions, cooperatives hold about as much capital as banks.\par}
\note{One and a half minutes. On average, banks hold much more regulatory capital: a mean of 31 percent of risk-weighted assets against 23 for cooperatives. At the median the two are close, 18 against 20. The difference between the averages comes from a tail of banks that are small or barely lend and sit on ratios above 50 percent, some above 100. Cooperatives stay in a narrow band. At the low end of the distribution, where supervisors look, cooperatives hold more capital than banks at the same point. If asked about size: comparing at equal size, cooperatives hold about 16 points less at the mean and about 2 less at the median, so the conclusion is the same, a large average gap and a small typical one.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Result 3. Provisioning and margin depend on the comparison group}
\vspace{0.1cm}
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{s_group_ladder}}
\vspace{0.05cm}
{\scriptsize\color{muted}Cooperatives minus each comparison group, size and quarter controls (2), with 95 percent intervals. Filled: $p<0.05$.\par}
\vspace{0.2cm}
{\centering\small Against all non-cooperatives, cooperatives provision less and earn a thinner margin. That group includes non-bank lenders, such as consumer-finance companies, with high rates and heavy provisioning. Against any definition of a bank, neither holds.\par}
\note{One minute. A common claim is that cooperatives provision less and earn thinner margins. That holds only against every non-cooperative institution, a group that includes 158 entities with no loans and no deposits and many non-bank lenders. Against banks, commercial banks or deposit-funded banks, the provisioning gap is zero, and the margin gap is zero or, against deposit-funded banks, positive: about two points wider for cooperatives. The comparison group is therefore part of the result.}
\end{frame}

\note{One minute. One result remains open. Nationally the lending gap is large. Inside the same region it is gone. Either cooperatives lend more because they locate where lending is needed, which makes region a mediator, or region is a confounder. With eighteen control banks, the within-region test also has little power. Both estimates are reported.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Brazil matches the literature except on profitability}
\vspace{0.1cm}
{\footnotesize\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.3cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.8cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.4cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.3cm}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Theory expects} & \textbf{Brazil, this paper} & \textbf{Cross-country evidence}\\
\midrule
Capital & Less, in a narrower band & Less on average, narrower band; the typical cooperative holds about as much & Lower capitalisation: \textcolor{coop}{agrees}\\
Return volatility & Lower & Lower & Lower: \textcolor{coop}{agrees}\\
Profitability & No sign committed & Higher for the typical cooperative, before and after tax & Lower: \textcolor{bank}{differs}\\
Provisioning, margin & No difference & None against banks & --\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\par}
\vspace{0.3cm}
{\small The difference on profitability is narrower than it looks: part of the after-tax gap is the tax exemption, the average before-tax gap is not established, and the sample holds the largest cooperatives.\par}
\vspace{0.15cm}
{\scriptsize\color{muted}Theory: Cook (1995), Fonteyne (2007). Cross-country: Hesse and \v{C}ih\'ak (2007); Germany: Beck et al. (2009).\par}
\note{One minute. The expectations were stated before the results. Theory says cooperatives should hold less capital, in a narrower band, and have steadier returns, with no prediction on profitability. All of that holds in Brazil. The cross-country study by Hesse and \v{C}ih\'ak finds the same on capital and stability, and Beck and coauthors find cooperatives farther from insolvency in Germany. Where Brazil differs is profitability: abroad, cooperative stability came with lower profitability; here the typical cooperative earns more. That difference is narrower than it looks, because part of it is the tax exemption, the average before-tax gap is not established, and our sample is the largest cooperatives.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}
\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.32\textwidth}\card{3.9cm}{1. Profile}{At the same size, cooperatives lend more, run leaner, earn more and have steadier returns. For the typical cooperative, most of the after-tax profit gap is already there before tax.}
\column{0.32\textwidth}\card{3.9cm}{2. Capital}{The typical cooperative holds about as much capital as the typical bank. The average gap comes from a bank tail that cooperatives do not have.}
\column{0.32\textwidth}\card{3.9cm}{3. Comparison group}{Lower provisioning and thinner margins appear only against a group that includes non-bank lenders. Against banks, no difference.}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.48\textwidth}\card{5.8cm}{For regulators}{A benchmark set on the average institution describes a lending cooperative poorly. The full distribution is the better guide for calibration. The results do not by themselves support lighter rules.}
\column{0.48\textwidth}\card{5.8cm}{Limits}{The estimates are conditional correlations at equal size. The sample is the largest tenth of Brazilian cooperatives. Code and data pipeline are public.}
\end{columns}
\note{One minute. Three results and two caveats. For regulators, the average full-methodology institution is a poor benchmark for a lender, because that average is pulled by banks that do not lend. Calibration should consider the whole distribution; the results do not by themselves support lighter rules. The estimates are conditional correlations and describe the largest cooperatives. Thank you.}
\end{frame}
