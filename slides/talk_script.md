## 1. Title [0:20]

Good afternoon. I am Arthur Nery, from the Organization of Brazilian Cooperatives, the OCB System. This paper is joint work with Thiago Victorino and Rodrigo Rangel. It compares Brazilian credit cooperatives with banks that follow exactly the same prudential rules, and it has implications for the current debate on proportional regulation.

## 2. Prudential framework [0:45]

On one side we have credit cooperatives. They are owned by their members and lend mostly to their members. On the other side, commercial banks, owned by shareholders and lending to the general public.

The one rule that differs is tax. A cooperative's operations with its own members are not taxed. Banks pay income tax and the social contribution, around 35 percent.

Everything else is the same. Above a size threshold, a cooperative follows the same capital definition, the same minimum capital ratios, the same risk weights and the same provisioning table as a bank. So the rulebook is held fixed, and we can ask what remains different about the cooperative form.

## 3. Sample and comparison group [0:50]

We take every institution under the full Basel methodology from 2017 to 2024, from the Central Bank's public filings: 130 credit cooperatives, 144 banks, and 339 other non-cooperative institutions.

Each dot is one institution at its median over the period. The horizontal axis is credit over total assets, the vertical axis is funds raised over total assets. Cooperatives cluster together, with high credit and high funds raised. Banks are more spread out. Many of the other institutions sit at zero on both axes: they hold no loans and raise no funds.

Against that group, the differences in lending and funding would be mechanical. So the main comparison is with banks.

## 4. Four specifications [0:45]

This is a descriptive study. Cooperative status does not change over time, so we cannot claim that a cooperative behaves differently because it is a cooperative. What we can do is measure the gaps, with increasing stringency.

Specification one is the raw comparison. Two adds controls for size, measured as log total assets, and for the quarter. Three matches each cooperative to banks in the same size bracket. Four keeps only the size range where both groups exist, which drops the largest banks.

The top panel shows how the size distributions change. The bottom panel shows the fitted lines, and the arrow is the cooperative differential in after-tax return: about two points in every specification. A result counts only if it holds in all four.

## 5. Lending [0:30]

Cooperatives hold 17 to 19 points more of their assets in loans than banks of the same size. The typical cooperative has 59 percent of its assets in loans, the typical bank 40. Credit share rises with size and banks are larger, so the raw comparison slightly understates the gap.

## 6. Volatility [0:25]

Cooperative returns are steadier. Volatility is the standard deviation of each institution's return over the period, so we keep institutions with at least eight quarters. The gap is 1.1 points in the raw comparison and 1.7 to 1.9 points at equal size.

## 7. Z-score [0:30]

The z-score, as in Hesse and Čihák (pronounced "CHEE-hahk"), counts how many standard deviations of return it would take to exhaust equity. Cooperatives sit further from insolvency in every specification. Their equity ratio is lower at equal size, so the advantage comes mainly from steadier returns.

## 8. Return after tax [0:20]

After tax, cooperatives earn 1.6 to 2.1 points of assets more a year than banks of the same size, in every specification.

## 9. Return before tax [0:25]

Since tax is the one rule that differs, we look before tax. The gap is still positive but smaller, and it is significant only in the first two specifications. So on the average, the before-tax gap is not established.

## 10. Taxation [0:50]

At the median the picture is clearer. Before tax, the typical cooperative earns 3.2 percent of assets and a bank of the same size 1.8. After tax, 2.8 against 1.1. So most of the gap is already there before tax; the exemption widens it.

Why are cooperatives exempt? The law treats a cooperative as its members acting together. The surplus on operations with members belongs to them; operations with non-members are taxed. The median effective tax rate is about 1 percent for cooperatives and 35 percent for banks.

Two points when comparing that surplus with a bank's profit. Part of it is what members paid above cost, which can come back to them later. And what is retained goes largely to reserves that cannot be distributed. That is the main way a cooperative builds capital, since it cannot issue shares to outside investors.

## 11. Result 1: profile [0:40]

Here are all eleven outcomes together, at comparable size. Four are stable: cooperatives lend more, earn more after tax, have steadier returns and sit further from insolvency. Three keep their sign in every specification, but a tail of banks drives the size of the gap: cooperatives hold less capital, are more leveraged and have a lower cost-to-income ratio. The before-tax return, funding, provisioning and margin do not pass our rule.

## 12. Result 2: capital [0:45]

On average, banks hold much more regulatory capital: 31 percent of risk-weighted assets against 23 for cooperatives. At the median, the two are close: 20 for cooperatives, 18 for banks. The average is pulled up by a tail of banks that are small or barely lend, with ratios above 50 percent. So the typical cooperative holds about as much capital as the typical bank, and policy should look at the whole distribution.

## 13. Result 3: comparison group [0:40]

A common claim is that cooperatives provision less and earn thinner margins. That holds only against all non-cooperatives, a group that includes non-bank lenders with high rates and heavy provisioning. Against any definition of a bank, the provisioning gap disappears, and the margin gap is zero or even favours cooperatives. The comparison group is part of the result.

## 14. Region [0:35]

One question stays open. Cooperatives are concentrated in the South, banks in the Southeast. Nationally, cooperatives hold about 17 points more of their assets in loans. Within the same region, the gap disappears, but only about eighteen banks serve as controls. Either region is part of how cooperatives lend more, or it explains the gap. Our data cannot separate the two.

## 15. Literature [0:35]

Theory expects cooperatives to hold less capital, in a narrower band, and to have steadier returns, with no prediction on profitability. All of that holds in Brazil, and cross-country studies find the same on capital and stability. The difference is profitability: abroad, cooperatives are less profitable; here the typical cooperative earns more, before and after tax. On provisioning and margin we found no cross-country comparison.

## 16. Summary [0:40]

At the same size and under the same rules, cooperatives lend more, run leaner, earn more and have steadier returns. The typical cooperative holds about as much capital as the typical bank. Lower provisioning and thinner margins appear only against a group that includes non-bank lenders.

For regulators, a benchmark set on the average institution describes a lending cooperative poorly; the full distribution is a better guide. The results do not by themselves support lighter rules. These are conditional correlations, and the sample is the largest tenth of Brazilian cooperatives, the ones under the full rules.

## 17. For discussion [0:45]

To close, a look beyond Brazil. Some countries give cooperatives a separate regime: credit unions in the US, the UK and Ireland, and savings cooperatives in Mexico. Others keep the same rules but adapt capital: in the EU, member shares count as core capital and a cooperative network can be supervised as one unit. And some, like Brazil, tier only by size.

The EU and the UK are now preparing simpler rules for small banks, defined by size. Brazil suggests that size alone may not separate cooperatives from banks. I would welcome your views, especially from other countries. Thank you.
